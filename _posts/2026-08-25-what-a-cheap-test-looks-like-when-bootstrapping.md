A recent post argued that the biggest mistake indie hackers make is shipping new products while the last one still struggles. [I pushed back](https://x.com/calltheball_/status/2092232959738749381).

I prefer a barbell strategy: spend about 90% of your effort on the project that has already shown signs of working, and use the other 10% to run cheap tests on new ideas.

But what counts as "cheap"? A test can cost almost nothing in cash and still waste weeks of your time. It helps to put numbers on it.

## Start with two rough numbers

First, estimate the value of the project. Say you think it has a 5% chance of producing $100,000:

> `5% × $100,000 = $5,000`

That is a rough estimate, not a valuation. Its purpose is to give you something to update as you learn.

Second, put a price on your time. If your main job pays $100,000 a year, dividing that by roughly 2,000 working hours gives you an hourly rate of $50. Ten hours spent on a side project therefore costs about $500 in time.

The same applies if you already run a micro-SaaS making $5,000 a month. If ten hours spent improving it would normally protect or add around $500 of value, then a ten-hour experiment elsewhere has a $500 opportunity cost.

These estimates will be imperfect. That is fine. Treating your time as free is much less accurate.

## Price the next test

Suppose a test takes ten hours and costs another $500 for ads, data or a prototype. At an hourly rate of $50, its real cost is:

> `$500 cash + (10 hours × $50) = $1,000`

Now suppose fully pursuing the project would require another $5,000, and a successful outcome would be worth $100,000.

Before the test, you believe the chance of success is 5%. The project's expected gross value is therefore $5,000, exactly equal to the cost of pursuing it. There is no margin of safety.

The test could change the decision. You estimate an 80% chance of a weak result, which would reduce the probability of success to about 1%. You would stop. There is also a 20% chance of a strong result, which would raise it to 20%. In that case:

> `20% × $100,000 = $20,000 expected gross value`

After subtracting the $5,000 needed to pursue the project, the strong result is worth $15,000. Because you expect that result 20% of the time:

> `20% × $15,000 = $3,000`

Finally, subtract the $1,000 cost of the test:

> `$3,000 - $1,000 = $2,000 expected value`

The test does not magically create $2,000 in profit. Its value comes from the decision it enables: abandon the idea when the evidence is weak, and make the larger bet only when the evidence improves.

## The 2/20/200 rule

This is the logic behind a simple progression:

- Spend 2 hours to decide whether another 20 are worthwhile.
- Spend 20 hours to decide whether another 200 are worthwhile.
- Spend 200 hours to decide whether to commit seriously.

At each stage, ask whether the next step is worth the information it can give you. A cheap test has a capped downside and a decision attached to it. If the result will not change what you do next, the information is not worth paying for.
